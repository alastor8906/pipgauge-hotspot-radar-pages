# One Day Before the ECB Decision, Recalculate the Trade Instead of Predicting It

With the July 23 ECB decision one day away, the familiar temptation was to spend all the preparation time trying to predict it.

That dated setup now belongs to a historical pre-event scenario, but the risk lesson remains useful.

Will the ECB sound more concerned about inflation? Will growth risks dominate? Will EUR/USD jump or fall?

Even a correct policy forecast can produce the wrong trade. Markets react to the difference between the announcement and what was already priced in, not simply to whether a rate moved.

A more controllable preparation step is to recalculate the position for event-day conditions.

## The same chart setup can carry different event risk

Suppose a trader normally uses a 25-pip stop on EUR/USD. Ahead of a policy decision, that stop may feel too tight, so it is widened to 50 or 100 pips.

That can be reasonable as a scenario adjustment. The mistake is keeping the original lot size.

For a $10,000 account with a 1% risk budget, the planned loss is $100. In a USD account, one standard EUR/USD lot is approximately $10 per pip.

The position sizes are therefore:

- 25-pip stop: 0.40 lot
- 50-pip stop: 0.20 lot
- 100-pip stop: 0.10 lot

Each version has the same $100 planned stop loss. The chart distance changes, but the account-level risk does not.

If the 0.40-lot position were kept with a 100-pip stop, the planned loss would become $400. A decision that looked like “giving the trade more room” would actually quadruple the dollar exposure.

## Spread widening is a separate cost

The stop calculation is not the whole trade.

If the spread is 0.8 pip, entering 0.40 lot costs roughly $3.20 before commission. If the spread widens to 3 pips, the same entry costs about $12.

That does not mean every ECB decision will produce a 3-pip spread. It means the cost assumption should be checked rather than copied from an ordinary session.

Reducing the position also reduces that spread cost. At 0.10 lot, a 3-pip spread is approximately $3.

This is why stop distance, position size and spread should be examined together even though they represent different things:

- Stop distance defines the planned price room.
- Position size converts that distance into account risk.
- Spread adds a transaction cost before the directional result begins.

## A stop is a request, not a guaranteed price

During a fast repricing, a stop can fill beyond its trigger. That is slippage. The clean $100 calculation assumes execution at the planned level, so it is a baseline rather than a guarantee.

The possibility of slippage is not a reason to abandon arithmetic. It is a reason to avoid using every dollar of the maximum loss limit in the arithmetic.

Some traders handle event risk by reducing size below their normal budget. Others stay flat. The important point is that the choice is made explicitly, not hidden inside an unchanged lot size.

## A five-minute pre-event check

Before a major decision, the following questions are more useful than a confident directional guess:

1. What is the maximum dollar loss I am willing to accept?
2. Has the stop distance changed from the original setup?
3. Does the lot size still match that dollar budget?
4. What spread am I assuming, and is it realistic for the event window?
5. What happens if the fill is worse than the stop price?
