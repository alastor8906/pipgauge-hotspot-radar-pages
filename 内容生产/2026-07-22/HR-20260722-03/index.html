Two Earnings Reports Can Turn Two Positions Into One Risk Event

Tesla and Alphabet were scheduled to report after the close on the same day. That creates an obvious news story, but the more useful question for anyone already holding both names is not which report will be better.

It is this:

If both positions move against the account at once, how much money is actually exposed?

The question matters because two ticker symbols can create the impression of diversification. On a shared earnings night, however, both positions may react to the same broad themes: technology spending, AI expectations, index flows and overall risk appetite. Separate trade tickets do not guarantee separate outcomes.

Start with notional exposure

Consider a $10,000 account with two positions:

  • $2,500 of Tesla exposure
  • $2,500 of Alphabet exposure
  • $5,000 of combined notional exposure

These are round numbers for illustrating the arithmetic. They are not suggested position sizes and they do not forecast either stock.

Now apply the same adverse gap to both positions:

  • At 5%, each position loses $125. Combined loss: $250, or 2.5% of the account.
  • At 10%, each position loses $250. Combined loss: $500, or 5% of the account.
  • At 15%, each position loses $375. Combined loss: $750, or 7.5% of the account.

Nothing in that table predicts a 15% move. Its purpose is to reveal what the existing positions would do if a large move occurred.

That distinction is important. Scenario analysis is not a forecast. It is a way to make the account consequence visible before the headline arrives.

Why a normal stop may not contain an earnings gap

During regular trading, a stop order may provide a useful reference for planned risk. After-hours news can cause the next tradable price to appear beyond that stop.

If a stock closes at $100, a stop at $95 expresses a plan to limit the loss to roughly 5%. It does not guarantee a fill at $95 if the first available price after the report is $88.

That is why earnings exposure needs two calculations:

  1. Planned risk, based on the intended exit or normal stop.
  2. Gap risk, based on a larger adverse move that may jump over the planned exit.

A portfolio can look acceptable under the first calculation and uncomfortable under the second.

Correlation changes when the catalyst is shared

Suppose one position rises 10% while the other falls 10%. With equal $2,500 notionals, the simplified net change is close to zero before costs. That outcome is possible, but it should not be assumed.

If both stocks fall 10%, the account loses $500. If one position is twice the size of the other, the larger position will dominate the result even if the percentage moves are identical.

The real exposure therefore depends on three things:

  • The dollar size of each position
  • The size and direction of each gap
  • Whether the positions respond independently or to the same market narrative

Counting tickers answers none of those questions.

Set one budget for the event cluster

A cleaner approach is to choose a total event-risk budget before deciding the size of either position.

For a $10,000 account, a trader might decide that the two-report cluster should not model more than $100 of planned loss. That $100 can be split equally, or allocated differently if one setup has a wider adverse-move assumption.

The sequence is:

  1. Set the maximum combined dollar loss for the event.
  2. Allocate that amount across the two positions.
  3. Calculate each position from its own adverse-move scenario.
  4. Add a second, larger gap scenario for both names moving together.
  5. Reduce or remove exposure if the stress result is not acceptable.

The important part is that the portfolio limit comes first. Calculating two positions independently and adding them later can quietly double the risk that felt acceptable on each individual ticket.

Notional value is not the same as portfolio heat

The example holds $5,000 of combined notional exposure. That does not mean the planned loss is $5,000, and it does not mean the maximum possible loss is limited to the stop.

Notional exposure tells you how much market value is represented. Portfolio heat tells you how much the account is expected to lose if the defined risk scenarios occur together.

For the 5% scenario, the two $2,500 positions create $250 of modeled heat. For the 15% scenario, they create $750. The holdings did not change; only the scenario changed.

This is why portfolio heat should always carry an assumption label. A single percentage without the scenario behind it can create false precision.

A practical check before a shared catalyst

Before holding multiple positions through the same reporting window, write down:

  • Account balance
  • Dollar value of each position
  • Planned loss for each position
  • A larger overnight-gap scenario
  • Combined loss if both positions move against the account
  • Whether adding after a gap is permitted

The arithmetic can be done in a spreadsheet. I used the PositionMath portfolio heat calculator to place both positions in one view after working through the scenarios. The tool is useful for organizing the exposure, but it does not know what either company will report.

The most useful result is not a prediction. It is the moment when two apparently separate trades become one visible account-level number.
