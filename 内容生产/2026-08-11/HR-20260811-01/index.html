RBA Held at 4.35%. What Should Change in an AUD/USD Risk Plan?

The Reserve Bank of Australia unanimously left its cash rate target at 4.35% on August 11. Its statement said inflation remained too high, described policy as somewhat restrictive, and kept open the possibility of another increase if upside risks materialised.

The immediate currency reaction was quieter than the headline. FOREX.com reported that AUD/USD was less than 0.1% below its previous close at the time of its analysis. That is a useful observation, but it is not a forecast. A muted first move does not promise a quiet next session, especially with another scheduled data event approaching.

The practical question is therefore not whether the Australian dollar must rise or fall after a hold. It is how to keep the same account risk when a trader considers different stop distances.

One dollar budget, three possible stops

Assume a $10,000 USD account and a maximum planned loss of 0.5%, or $50. For AUD/USD in a USD account, one standard lot is approximately $10 per pip.

The basic formula is:

lot size = dollar risk / (stop distance in pips × pip value per standard lot)

With a $50 risk budget:

  • A 20-pip stop gives 50 / (20 × 10) = 0.25 lot.
  • A 35-pip stop gives 50 / (35 × 10) = 0.1429 lot, or about 0.14 lot before broker rounding.
  • A 50-pip stop gives 50 / (50 × 10) = 0.10 lot.

Each row targets the same $50 planned loss. The market view can stay unchanged while the appropriate size changes. If the stop widens from 20 to 50 pips and the position remains 0.25 lot, the planned loss rises from $50 to $125 before trading costs.

Low initial volatility is not a sizing rule

The reported sub-0.1% AUD/USD move describes one point in time after the decision. It does not establish the range for the rest of the day, the next session, or the next data release. Using it as permission to increase leverage would turn a descriptive market observation into an unsupported prediction.

A risk plan needs inputs that the trader controls: account currency, maximum dollar loss, stop distance, and instrument size. It should treat spread, commission and slippage separately. A planned stop is not a guaranteed fill, and the arithmetic above excludes execution costs.

For example, a trader using 0.25 lot might also test a hypothetical extra two pips of adverse cost. At roughly $2.50 per pip for that position, the extra cost would be about $5. This is a stress scenario, not a claim about the spread available during the RBA announcement.

What the RBA facts do—and do not—tell us

The official decision supplies the news hook: 4.35%, a unanimous vote, and an inflation outlook that keeps future policy conditional. The Statement on Monetary Policy supplies the broader economic context. Neither document specifies an AUD/USD target, a stop distance, or a correct position size for an individual account.

That boundary matters. A rate decision can change volatility, correlations and market expectations. It cannot decide how much money an account should risk. Position sizing should begin with the loss budget and work backward.

Before placing a trade around a scheduled event, a compact checklist is enough:

  1. Set the maximum dollar loss.
  2. Choose a stop from the trade structure, not from the lot size you want.
  3. Recalculate the lot size whenever the stop changes.
  4. Add a separate cost and slippage stress case.
  5. Recheck all time-sensitive wording after the next data release.

The pip-value and lot-size arithmetic can be verified with PipGauge's position size calculator. The tool checks inputs; it does not forecast AUD/USD or guarantee execution.

The RBA decision may change the market conversation. It does not need to change the amount an account is prepared to lose.

RBA held at 4.35% — do you change size when the first AUD/USD move is quiet?

The RBA unanimously held the cash rate at 4.35%. One market report described AUD/USD as moving less than 0.1% from the previous close at the time, but I do not think that initial reaction is a reason to assume the rest of the session will stay quiet.

The more useful question seems to be whether the same dollar-risk rule survives a different stop.

Example: $10k USD account, maximum planned loss $50, and AUD/USD at roughly $10 per pip for one standard lot.

  • 20-pip stop: 0.25 lot
  • 35-pip stop: about 0.14 lot
  • 50-pip stop: 0.10 lot

All three target the same $50 loss before costs. Keeping 0.25 lot while widening from 20 to 50 pips would instead raise planned risk to $125.

This is not a prediction about the Australian dollar, and the first move after the announcement does not tell us the next range. Spread and slippage also need a separate stress case.

For people who trade scheduled decisions: do you reduce size automatically, widen the stop and recalculate, or simply skip the first window? I am interested in the risk process, not directional calls.
