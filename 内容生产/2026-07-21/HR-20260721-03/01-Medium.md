# Gold Near $4,000 Before the First 24/7 Weekend Session

On July 21, gold was trading around $4,000 while CME's first weekend window for its expanded 1-Ounce Gold Futures schedule was approaching. The combination produced a simple headline: “gold is going 24/7.” The product details were less simple.

CME's expansion applies to a specific instrument: the 1-Ounce Gold Futures contract. A retail trader looking at XAUUSD through a CFD broker may see a similar gold price, but that does not make the products interchangeable.

They can differ in contract size, venue, trading hours, margin rules, transaction costs and how a platform defines a “pip.”

That distinction matters more than the headline.

## Start with the product, not the symbol

A futures contract is standardized by its exchange. Its specifications define how much of the underlying it represents and when it trades.

A CFD is an agreement with a broker. The broker publishes the contract size, minimum lot, spread, leverage and trading schedule. A common retail XAUUSD convention is 100 troy ounces per 1.00 lot, but traders should not assume that convention without checking the broker's specification page.

The word “gold” is therefore not enough to calculate risk. You need to know which contract you actually hold.

## The same $25 move can mean $25 or $2,500

Assume a retail XAUUSD contract uses 100 ounces per standard lot. If gold moves by $25 per ounce, the approximate gross change is:

- 0.01 lot represents 1 ounce: about $25
- 0.10 lot represents 10 ounces: about $250
- 1.00 lot represents 100 ounces: about $2,500

For a 1-Ounce Gold Futures contract, the same $25 move corresponds to roughly $25 in contract value before costs.

The price move is the same. The exposure unit is not.

This is also why statements such as “gold moved 250 pips” can create confusion. Some retail platforms treat a gold pip as $0.01; others use $0.10 or display points differently. A dollar price move and the contract's ounce exposure are usually clearer than a bare pip count.

## More trading hours do not guarantee more liquidity

Continuous access and continuous liquidity are different things.

An instrument may be available for trading while the order book is thinner than it is during the most active weekday sessions. A thinner market can mean wider spreads and more price impact for an order.

That does not make 24/7 access inherently good or bad. It means “open” should not be read as “equally liquid at every hour.”

The same caution applies to retail CFDs. A broker's published trading schedule and its actual spread conditions are separate inputs to a risk decision.

## A cleaner way to compare gold exposure

Before comparing two gold products, write down five fields:

1. Product and venue
2. Ounces represented by one contract or lot
3. Minimum trade size
4. Trading schedule
5. Spread, commission and margin method

Only then convert a price move into money:

`gross P&L = price change per ounce × ounces represented`

For the retail CFD examples above, I used PipGauge's [XAUUSD pip value calculator](https://pipgauge.com/xauusd-pip-value-calculator/) to check both common pip conventions. It is deliberately limited to broker-style XAUUSD inputs; it should not be presented as a CME futures calculator.

The durable lesson is not whether gold trades around the clock. It is that a familiar ticker and a familiar price can still hide a different contract underneath.
