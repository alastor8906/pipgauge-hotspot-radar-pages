# Australia’s July Jobs Report Is Out. Recheck AUD/USD Risk With the Actual Numbers

Australia’s July labour-force result is now public. Seasonally adjusted employment fell by 15,800 and the unemployment rate rose to 4.5%. Reuters independently reported the result, including a revised June employment gain of 80,300 and the prior market expectation of a 15,000 increase.

Those facts explain why readers are looking at the report. They do not provide an AUD/USD direction, a correct stop distance, or a position size.

The practical question is narrower: if the amount an account is willing to lose stays fixed, how must size change when the planned stop is wider?

## Actual data is not a trading instruction

The Australian Bureau of Statistics reports the July figures. The 15,000 number was a forecast before release; it is not the actual result. Neither the employment miss nor the 4.5% unemployment rate tells a trader where AUD/USD has to go next, how far it could move, or whether a stop will fill at its intended price.

That is why the risk calculation should begin with a controllable number: the maximum planned dollar loss.

## One loss budget, three stop distances

Assume a USD 10,000 account with a maximum planned loss of 1%, or USD 100. For AUD/USD in a USD account, one standard lot is approximately USD 10 per pip.

`lot size = dollar risk / (stop distance in pips × pip value per standard lot)`

- 25-pip stop: `100 / (25 × 10) = 0.40 lot`.
- 50-pip stop: `100 / (50 × 10) = 0.20 lot`.
- 75-pip stop: `100 / (75 × 10) = 0.1333 lot`, or about 0.13 lot before broker rounding.

Each example targets the same USD 100 planned price loss before costs. Keep 0.40 lot while moving a stop from 25 to 75 pips, and planned price risk rises from USD 100 to USD 300. That is a sizing consequence, not an Australia-jobs forecast.

## Separate the report from the inputs you control

1. State the released facts and the old forecast separately.
2. Set the maximum dollar loss before choosing lot size.
3. Choose the stop from the trade structure, rather than from a desired lot size.
4. Recalculate if the stop changes.
5. Treat spread, commission and slippage as a separate hypothetical stress case. A planned stop is not a guaranteed fill.

For example, 0.20 lot has an approximate pip value of USD 2. At a 50-pip stop it targets USD 100 of price risk before costs. A hypothetical extra two pips of adverse cost adds about USD 4; this is a stress calculation, not a claim about live execution.
