Tesla and Alphabet Report on the Same Day. How Much Portfolio Risk Is That?

Two separate stock positions can become one concentrated event bet when their earnings arrive together.

Tesla and Alphabet are both scheduled to report second-quarter results after the market closes on July 22.

It is easy to look at the positions separately: one Tesla trade and one Alphabet trade, each with its own chart, thesis and stop.

At the portfolio level, however, both positions are exposed to a high-volatility event on the same afternoon. If the market reacts to the broader technology or AI narrative, the losses may arrive together.

The useful question is not only how much to risk on each stock. It is how much the account is risking across both positions at the same time.

Give the event a total risk budget

Imagine a $10,000 account. The trader decides that the combined earnings exposure should not model more than 1% of the account, or $100.

One simple allocation is:

  • Tesla risk budget: $50
  • Alphabet risk budget: $50
  • Total planned event risk: $100

The split does not have to be equal. The important part is setting the portfolio cap before calculating either position size.

Without a cap, two trades that each look modest can quietly double the intended account risk.

A hypothetical share-size example

Use round numbers only to show the method, not to forecast the stocks.

Assume:

  • Tesla reference price: $300
  • Alphabet reference price: $200
  • Planned adverse move for each position: 8%
  • Maximum modeled loss per position: $50

An 8% move in Tesla is $24 per share. The risk-based share count is:

$50 / $24 = 2.08 shares

Rounding down gives 2 shares, with about $48 of modeled loss.

An 8% move in Alphabet is $16 per share:

$50 / $16 = 3.125 shares

Rounding down gives 3 shares, also with about $48 of modeled loss.

Together, the two positions model roughly $96 of loss under the 8% scenario.

The useful output is not the precise share count. It is the visibility: the two trades consume nearly the full $100 event budget.

Earnings gaps can exceed the planned move

A stop order cannot guarantee an exit price when a stock gaps after hours or opens away from the previous session's range.

Suppose both stocks fall 15% instead of the modeled 8%.

For the same hypothetical prices and share counts:

  • Tesla: 2 shares x $45 = about $90 loss
  • Alphabet: 3 shares x $30 = about $90 loss
  • Combined: about $180 loss

The 1% plan becomes a 1.8% outcome.

That does not make the original risk calculation useless. It shows why event risk needs an additional gap scenario and why position sizes may need to be smaller than a normal stop-based calculation suggests.

Portfolio heat is different from capital invested

The dollar value of the positions is:

  • Tesla: 2 x $300 = $600
  • Alphabet: 3 x $200 = $600
  • Total capital exposure: $1,200

But the planned portfolio heat under the 8% scenario is about $96.

Capital invested answers how much market value the account holds. Portfolio heat answers how much is expected to be lost if the defined risk scenarios occur together.

Both are useful. They should not be confused.

Check the positions together, not one at a time

The PositionMath portfolio heat calculator is useful here because both positions can be entered into the same risk view. The position size calculator can then work backward from each stock's allocated dollar risk.

The calculator does not predict the earnings reaction. It provides a place to test several scenarios:

  • Normal planned move
  • Larger overnight gap
  • One stock down while the other is unchanged
  • Both stocks moving against the portfolio together

The last case is the one that separate trade tickets tend to hide.

Do not use averaging down to erase the original risk budget

If a stock gaps down, adding shares lowers the average entry price but increases capital and risk exposure.

That can be a deliberate new decision, but it should not be treated as a repair that makes the initial loss disappear. Any additional purchase should be evaluated against a new portfolio-level risk cap, including the remaining position in the other stock.

A practical earnings-event checklist

Before holding multiple stocks through the same reporting window:

  1. Set one maximum dollar loss for the whole event cluster.
  2. Allocate that budget across the positions.
  3. Calculate share counts from an explicit adverse-move scenario.
  4. Add a larger gap scenario that can overrun the stop.
  5. Check whether the positions share the same sector, factor or market narrative.
  6. Decide in advance whether adding after a gap is allowed.

Two tickers do not always create two independent risks. On a shared catalyst day, they can behave like one larger position.
